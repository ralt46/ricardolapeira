# Underhyped Technologies

### Project: Foresight Research & Strategic Recommendations for the UK's Frontier Tech Hub

*Developed with [Pluriversa](https://pluriversa.org/en/underhyped-tech-for-post-development/), a Latin American consultancy for ecosocial transitions, commissioned by the UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office through its Frontier Tech Hub.*

**Foresight & Scenario Planning · Research Strategy · Content Strategy · Stakeholder Engagement**

---

Most of the work in this portfolio is about helping a product, an app, or an organisation communicate clearly with the people who depend on it. This project asked the same question at a much larger scale: how do you take a mountain of technical research and turn it into something a government, a funder, or a community can actually use to make a decision?

The client this time was the UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office, through its Frontier Tech Hub. Underneath the policy language, the problem was a familiar one: too much information, and not enough clarity about what actually mattered.

### The Challenge

They wanted an answer that went beyond the usual technology-trend report. The global conversation about emerging tech tends to concentrate on high-hype, high-return narratives, which leaves out technologies with real social, ecological, and territorial value: the kind that will never headline a conference keynote but could genuinely change how a community accesses water, energy, or its own data.

The brief was to find those underhyped technologies and turn that discovery into something usable: strategic, evidence-based recommendations that public institutions, private funders, and communities could act on, not just admire from a distance.

### My Role

I joined this project as Foresight & Ethics Lead, working alongside a small interdisciplinary team at Pluriversa. Part of my work centred on the research strategy itself: defining how we would decide what counted as "underhyped" in the first place, coordinating an international network of experts across multiple continents to pressure-test our thinking, and designing the research operations that kept a five-month, multi-country project on track.

But research that stays in a spreadsheet does not change anyone's decisions. A significant part of the work was translating dense, cross-continental research into the structured, decision-ready content that became the project's public knowledge platform: the selection criteria, the technology write-ups, the recommendations, all in language a funder or policymaker without a research background could pick up and actually use. It is the same discipline behind any content strategy work I do, just aimed at a government audience instead of a product's users.

### The Process

We started from a wide net: 142 technologies with the potential to matter to what this project calls post-development, essentially, progress defined by the people affected by it, rather than by whoever built the technology or whoever profits from it.

Narrowing that list meant asking two questions most tech-trend reports never bother with: attention (who is actually looking at this technology, and why) and potential (what could it change at a systemic, territorial level for the people closest to it, not just for a market). We tested our thinking against real technical and territorial experts throughout, specifically to catch our own assumptions and biases before they made it into a recommendation someone would act on.

From there, I facilitated the workshops that turned expert input and raw research into something a stakeholder could actually sit with and act on. That process, research, structure, then translation, is how a list of 142 became nine technologies worth genuine attention.

### The Decision

It would have been easier, and arguably more fundable, to build our selection criteria around the metric everyone already trusts: Technology Readiness Level, essentially how close something is to market. We chose not to.

As the ethics lead on this project, I pushed for criteria that measured something TRL cannot: whether a technology strengthens community autonomy or quietly erodes it, whether it keeps value inside a territory or extracts it, whether adoption depends on a handful of licensing gatekeepers or on open, community-controlled infrastructure. That is a harder case to make in a funding conversation than "how close is this to shipping." It is also the only honest way to answer the question the Frontier Tech Hub actually asked us: which technologies deserve more attention, and who should that attention actually serve?

### The Outcome

The project delivered, on schedule at five months and with full stakeholder approval, a public knowledge platform covering a portfolio of nine underhyped technologies, four plausible scenarios projected to 2035, and five sets of strategic recommendations covering everything from data sovereignty to community-centred business models.

**What started as a request for a technology trend report became a decision-making framework that public and private actors can use to fund and adopt technology more fairly, not just more efficiently.**

The work is public and documented in full through [Pluriversa](https://pluriversa.org/en/underhyped-tech-for-post-development/) and the [Frontier Tech Hub](https://www.frontiertechhub.org/underhyped-tech).
