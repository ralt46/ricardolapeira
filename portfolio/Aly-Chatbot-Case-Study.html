Shaping the Identity of a Conversational Chatbot

Project: UX Research, UX Writing, Testing & Conversational Design for a Global NGO

NOTE: some details are simplified to respect confidentiality agreements.

Most people have talked to a chatbot that gets the facts right and still somehow misses to answer correctly. It is able to find the correct information but fails to deliver it. That gap, between correct and actually helpful, is exactly where UX work on AI products lives.

This is the story of Aly, an AI-powered chatbot I helped build for Equimundo, an international NGO with ambitious plans to improve its processes.

The Challenge

Equimundo needed a way to reach its facilitators, educators, and program staff directly, wherever they were working. The goal was an always-on tool, built from scratch, that could help teams plan work sessions, brainstorm activities, and get real answers in the moment during a workshop, not a static FAQ page dressed up as a chatbot.

The ambition was real, and so were the constraints. The team had limited experience with digital tools in general, and none with AI specifically, but they had the will to make it happen. The people who would actually use Aly varied widely in digital literacy and worked across multiple languages and cultural contexts. If the tool did not meet people where they actually were, technically and emotionally, it risked not being used at all.

My Role

I owned the UX strategy for the project: user research, conversational flow design, workshop and survey design, tone of voice, and a major part of the architecture of the knowledge base the AI would draw from.

The Process

I started as I usually do: understanding the specific situations users found themselves in, and the exact moments where a tool like this could genuinely help rather than get in the way. That research is what defined the use cases the chatbot needed to support.

From there, I turned to something people often assume is a purely cosmetic decision: tone. Aly needed to feel respectful, friendly, and supportive across both Spanish and English, for users with very different levels of digital confidence. That is why I built the interaction guidelines around one specific insight: good prompts produce good answers. Rather than treating people's unfamiliarity with digital tools as a barrier to design around, we designed the experience to build their confidence from the very first message.

The Pivot

Then testing surfaced a problem no amount of good writing could fix. Across multiple locations, users kept hitting the same quality issues in the answers. Tests pointed to clear diagnostics that something was off. So, working with the development team and project leadership, we were able to trace it back to its root: the tech stack we had started with was not built for what we were actually asking it to do.

That is the part of this work I find genuinely interesting: not the moment something breaks, but the discipline of tracing a symptom back to its real cause instead of settling for the nearest convenient one.

Fixing it meant switching to a different LLM model, which meant rebuilding the entire knowledge base underneath it: how content was parsed, indexed, and retrieved. It was a hard call to make partway through a project with a deadline already set. It was also the right one. The team worked intensely to rebuild what needed rebuilding, and delivered on time, at the level of quality the project actually needed.

The Outcome

The tool improved considerably, validated across every use case through iterative testing with real users. The onboarding experience went smoothly, and the staff responded to a tool that felt built for them, one that reflected their daily challenges instead of a generic idea of what an AI assistant should sound like.

Why This Matters

Some AI tools are somewhat still new enough that most organizations get the sequencing backwards. They build the technology first and hope the experience follows. But an AI product succeeds or fails on the same terms any product does: whether it understands the person on the other side of it, and whether it earns their trust quickly enough for them to keep coming back.

That is the work I care about doing, regardless of what is powering a product on the inside: making sure the person actually using it feels understood, not managed.
