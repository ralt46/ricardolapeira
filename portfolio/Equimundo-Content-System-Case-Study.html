Architecting an Internal Knowledge Platform

Project: Content Strategy, DesignOps & Information Architecture for a Single-Source-of-Truth Platform

NOTE: some details are simplified to respect confidentiality agreements.

Every organization knows that at some point, they either undergo a digital transformation or their processes will eventually eat them alive.

Equimundo, an international NGO running programs across several countries, wanted to address this precise issue. They wanted to become more agile, more efficient, and most importantly, they wanted to serve better the people they worked for.

The Challenge

Equimundo needed to digitalize its workflows, but the real problem sat underneath that request. There was no centralized hub for the organization's processes or institutional knowledge. Information was scattered, operations varied significantly from one country team to another, and the knowledge that mattered most lived inside individual people rather than in any shared system.

The cost of that was concrete. Project managers spent real time just navigating fragmented resources to do their jobs, and every time someone left the organization, they took a piece of irreplaceable institutional memory with them. Equimundo knew it needed a tool to help them change. What it actually needed was a new way of thinking and working around its organizational knowledge.

My Role

I led the end-to-end design of a content strategy and DesignOps model for Equimundo, along with the processes and governance mechanisms behind it: UX research design and workshop facilitation, information architecture, content governance, and content creation and production, including AI-assisted workflows to help the platform scale across very different country contexts.

The Process

I designed and ran interviews across various levels of the organization, from frontline staff to senior management, and mapped how information actually moved day to day, and where it broke down.

That research surfaced something beyond the original brief: an opportunity to build proprietary upskilling content directly into the platform. This would allow team members in every country to access the same core skills, not just a folder of documents.

From there, I defined the content taxonomy to facilitate the browsing experience, built the information architecture to establish hierarchies within the existing information, and created the actual content that would live in the platform.

The Discovery

Most projects like this stop at the brief: build a place to put the documents, but then no one actually finds out what needs to be in there. I chose to treat the content as the actual deliverable, not something secondary. A knowledge hub nobody finds useful is of no value at all, and maintaining it ends up draining resources that could go somewhere else.

That is the engineer in me. A system does not deliver value just because it exists. It does so because it solves a specific problem. So alongside the architecture, I designed and wrote the information that users would have access to.

The Outcome

The platform launched as Equimundo's single source of truth: a centralized, structured knowledge hub accessible to team leaders, project managers, and staff across all countries. User testing shaped several rounds of improvement throughout the process.

After some tests, certain operational efficiencies started to emerge. These were directly linked to information access and knowledge management. Teams could now make use of best practices to improve their work.

I can gladly say the organization's confidence in the work went beyond this project. They later brought me back to design the experience for Aly, an AI-powered support chatbot built for their facilitators and educators, a story worth telling on its own.

Why This Matters

If your organization's institutional knowledge is scattered across inboxes, personal drives, and the memory of your longest-tenured staff, you already know how fragile that is. The fix is not just a wiki. It is a system with real structure and real ownership behind it, one that keeps working after the consultant leaves and the people who built it move on.

That is exactly the kind of problem I like solving: mapping how information actually moves through an organization, then building the structure and the rules that let it keep moving well.
