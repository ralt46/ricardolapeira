Tuily

Project: Brand Innovation, User Research & UX Writing for a FinTech Challenger

Some details are simplified to respect confidentiality agreements.

Most fintech products sound the same. Confident, modern, a little generic — the kind of voice that could belong to any company promising to "disrupt" an industry. Tuily could easily have ended up sounding like that too.

Instead, its voice was built around something more specific: a truth I found by actually talking to the people the product was for. That distinction, between a fintech voice and *this* fintech's actual reason to exist, became the foundation for everything I built across Tuily's product, content, and brand.

The Challenge

Tuily set out to do something genuinely difficult: bring modern financial tools — credit, cash flow management, day-to-day operational support — to small and medium businesses in a market built almost entirely for large ones. That is a hard sell on its own. It is harder still in a heavily regulated industry, where trust has to be earned before a single feature gets used.

When I joined, the product existed mostly as an idea and an early build with a developer-first mindset. There was no brand voice, no content strategy, and the copy that did exist had been written by the engineering team: inconsistent, error-prone, and quietly working against the experience it was supposed to support. Almost everything needed to be built, or rebuilt, from the ground up. UX was not something to add later. It needed to be part of the product from the inside out.

My Role

I owned the end-to-end user experience: strategy, research, testing, brand voice development, UX writing for the app, and content strategy across web, social, and advertising. I treated all of it as one connected system rather than a list of separate deliverables, because that is how a user actually experiences a brand: as one voice, whether they meet it in an ad, a signup form, or an error message.

The Process

I started with interviews, not with the interface. Talking to the small business owners Tuily was actually built for surfaced something that shaped everything that followed: these were not confused customers who didn't understand credit or cash flow. They understood both perfectly well. What they lacked was access. The same financial tools larger companies used without a second thought were, for them, consistently out of reach.

That insight became the narrative foundation for the entire product. Using card sorting and a user journey map, I translated it into a brand voice and a set of content guidelines, then applied them systematically across every touchpoint the user could encounter: the app, the website, social content, and advertising.

The Decision

The easier path would have been to write Tuily's voice around the same story every fintech tells about itself: fast, modern, ambitious. I chose not to. Every guideline, every piece of copy, every touchpoint was instead built around the specific pain the research had surfaced — exclusion, not ambition. It is a subtler story, and a riskier one to build a whole brand around, because it asks the product to actually deliver on it, rather than just sound impressive.

That question, whose problem is this actually solving, is one my background in philosophy trained me to ask before any design question. In this case, it is what turned a set of content guidelines into a narrative that meant something.

The Outcome

It paid off. The sales team reported that clients connected strongly with the narrative, not because it was clever, but because it reflected something real about their own experience running a business. **The product's story worked because it was built on an actual pain point, not an assumption about one.**

Tuily was later acquired by Bold, one of Latin America's leading fintech companies — validation not just of the product, but of the strategy behind how it spoke to the people it was built for.

"Working with Ricardo was a transformative experience. One of Ricardo's most significant contributions was his unparalleled understanding of content. He possesses an innate ability to dissect our brand's messaging needs and craft strategic UX content that speaks directly to our key target audiences." — Jaime Vengoechea

Why This Matters

If your product is technically solid but still is not connecting the way you would expect, the feature set is rarely the problem. It is usually the story you are telling about it, and whether that story is the one your users are actually living.

That is the kind of problem I like solving: finding the real insight underneath the assumptions, then building a voice and a strategy around it that holds up across every touchpoint, not just the pitch deck.
