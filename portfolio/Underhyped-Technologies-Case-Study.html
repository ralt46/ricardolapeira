Underhyped Technologies

Project: Foresight Research & Strategic Content

NOTE: some details are simplified to respect confidentiality agreements.

This project was developed with Pluriversa, a Latin American strategic design consultancy, commissioned by the UK's Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office (FCDO) through its Frontier Tech Hub.

Although similar to most of my projects, this one asked almost the same questions but at a much larger scale: how do you take a mountain of technical research and turn it into something a government, a funder, or a community can actually use to make a decision?

The audience this time were stakeholders with a high degree of technical expertise. Underneath the policy language, the problem was a familiar one: too much information, and not enough clarity about what actually mattered.

The Challenge

The client wanted an answer that went beyond the usual technology-trend report. The global conversation about emerging tech tends to concentrate on high-hype, high-return narratives, which leaves out technologies with real social, ecological, and territorial value: the kind that will never headline a conference keynote but could genuinely change how a community accesses water, energy, or its own data.

The brief was to find those underhyped technologies and turn that discovery into something usable: strategic, evidence-based recommendations that public institutions, private funders, and communities could act on, not just admire from a distance.

My Role

I joined this project as a foresight expert, working alongside a small interdisciplinary team at Pluriversa. Part of my work centred on the research strategy itself: defining how we would decide what counted as "underhyped" in the first place, coordinating an international network of experts across multiple continents to pressure-test our thinking, and designing the research operations that kept a five-month, multi-country project on track.

But research that stays in a spreadsheet does not change anyone's decisions. A significant part of the work was translating dense, cross-continental research into the structured, decision-ready content that became the project's public knowledge platform: the selection criteria, the write-ups, the recommendations, all in language a funder or policymaker could pick up and actually use. It was the same discipline behind any content strategy work I do, just aimed at a government audience instead of a product's users.

The Process

We started from a wide net: 142 technologies with the potential to make the cut. Narrowing that list meant asking two questions most tech-trend reports never bother with: attention (who is actually looking at this technology, and why) and potential (what could it change at a systemic, territorial level for the people closest to it, not just for a market). We tested our thinking against real technical and territorial experts throughout, specifically to catch our own assumptions and biases before they made it into a recommendation someone would act on.

From there, I helped facilitate the workshops that turned expert input and raw research into something a stakeholder could actually sit with and act on. That process, research, structure, then translation, is how the list of 142 became nine technologies worth genuine attention.

The Decision

It would have been easier to build our selection criteria around the metric everyone already trusts: Technology Readiness Level, essentially how close something is to market. We chose not to.

We pushed for criteria that measured something TRL cannot: whether a technology strengthens community autonomy or quietly erodes it, whether it keeps value inside a territory or extracts it, whether adoption depends on a handful of licensing gatekeepers or on open, community-controlled infrastructure. That is a harder case to make in a funding conversation than "how close is this to shipping." It is also the only honest way to answer the question the Frontier Tech Hub actually asked us: which technologies deserve more attention.

The Outcome

The project was delivered on schedule and with full approval. It was a public knowledge platform covering a portfolio of nine underhyped technologies, four plausible scenarios projected to 2035, and five sets of strategic recommendations covering everything from data sovereignty to community-centred business models.

What started as a potential request for a technology trend report became a decision-making framework that public and private actors can use to fund and adopt technology more fairly, not just more efficiently.

Why This Matters

Whether it is a digital product, an NGO's internal knowledge base, or a government-facing research platform, the underlying problem is the same: information does not do anything on its own. Someone has to turn it into something clear enough to act on.

As a content strategist, that is the work I care about, regardless of the audience: doing the research to know what is actually true, then building the content and structure that makes that truth usable for the people who need to act on it.
